The scientific method in six steps

Any project on this site can become a real investigation. Walk kids through these steps, out loud for young children or in a notebook for older ones.

  1. Ask a question. “Which liquid makes a penny shiniest?” A good question can be answered by testing.
  2. Do some research. What do you already know? Read the “What's going on?” section of the project.
  3. Make a prediction. Say or write what you think will happen and why. Being wrong is part of science.
  4. Test it fairly. Change only one thing at a time and keep everything else the same. Repeat tests if you can.
  5. Record and look at the results. Use tables, drawings, photos or a simple bar chart.
  6. Share what you found. Was the prediction right? What would you test next?

Safety rules to print and post

  1. Always have an adult nearby. Read the whole project, including the safety note, before you start.
  2. Wear safety goggles for anything that fizzes, splashes, launches or uses chemicals.
  3. Never taste or smell anything unless the project says it's food and a grown-up agrees.
  4. Adults handle hot water, stoves, ovens, flames, sharp knives and craft blades.
  5. Keep small parts, magnets, coin batteries and balloons away from babies and toddlers. They can be swallowed.
  6. Never use wall sockets for electricity projects. Batteries only.
  7. Check for allergies (nuts, latex balloons, foods) before starting.
  8. Never look at the Sun, even through sunglasses, binoculars or lenses.
  9. Work on a tray or covered table, and clean up and wash hands afterwards.
  10. Throw sealed bacteria and mold experiments away without opening them.

Making it work at home

  • Start with the kitchen. Kitchen Chemistry and Plants & Seeds projects use things most homes already have.
  • Let them lead. Ask “What do you think will happen?” before every step, and “Why do you think that happened?” after.
  • Plan for mess. A baking tray, old towel and clothes you don't mind ruining make everyone more relaxed.
  • Keep a lab notebook. Print the project page. It includes space for predictions and results.
  • Save favorites. Press “Save to favorites” on any project to build a list for weekends and holidays.

Running projects with a class

  • Prepare kits ahead. Use the “You will need” checklist to fill one tray per group.
  • Give each person a job. Reader, measurer, recorder and materials manager keep groups of four on track.
  • Demonstrate safety first. Show the goggles, the tray and the clean-up routine before handing anything out.
  • Turn it into a fair test. Give each group a different variable to change, then compare results as a class.
  • Use the extension. “Try this next” is a ready-made challenge for groups who finish early.
  • Show the video afterwards. Watching after the hands-on part keeps the surprise and helps kids check their method.

Science fair planner

Most projects here can grow into a science fair entry. A simple four-week plan:

  1. Week 1: Browse projects, pick one that sparks curiosity, and write a testable question.
  2. Week 2: Research, list materials, write a prediction and plan a fair test with one variable.
  3. Week 3: Run the experiment at least three times. Record everything and take photos.
  4. Week 4: Make charts, write a conclusion and build the display board.

A display board usually includes

Title and question, prediction (hypothesis), materials, method, results with charts and photos, conclusion, and what you'd test next.

Choosing projects by age

  • Ages 3 to 5: Short, sensory projects with a big “wow”, like Oobleck, Magic Milk, Sink or Float and Rain Clouds. Focus on noticing and describing.
  • Ages 6 to 8: Simple predictions and comparisons, like Walking Water, Bean in a Bag and Balloon Rockets. Start drawing results.
  • Ages 9 to 11: Fair tests with measurements, like Lemon Battery, Paper Bridge Strength and the Rusting Race. Make tables and bar charts.
  • Ages 12 to 14: Multi-day and engineering challenges, like Strawberry DNA, Mousetrap Car and the Water Bottle Rocket. Plan, repeat and analyze.
