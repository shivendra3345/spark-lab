# Paper Bridge Strength
e=🌉 a=6-12 d=Easy t=30 min
m: printer paper; 2 equal stacks of books; pennies; small cup; tape; ruler
- Make two book stacks 15 cm apart and lay one flat sheet of paper across the gap.
- Set a small cup in the middle and add pennies one at a time until the bridge sags into the gap. Record the number.
- Fold a new sheet into accordion pleats (like a fan) and test again.
- Try a third sheet rolled into a tube, then one folded into a U-shaped channel.
- Make a bar chart comparing how many pennies each shape held.
w: A flat sheet bends easily. Folding the same paper into pleats or tubes makes it stiffer because the material is spread farther from the middle, so it resists bending. Real bridges use I-beams and corrugated steel for the same reason.
x: Increase the gap to 25 cm. Which shape still works best?

# Cardboard Grabber Claw

# Earthquake-Proof Building
e=🏢 a=9-13 d=Medium t=1.5 hours
m: index cards; straws; toothpicks; mini marshmallows; tape; tray of firm gelatin (set overnight); small weights
- Build a tower at least 30 cm tall on top of the gelatin "ground".
- Place a small weight on the top floor to act like heavy rooftop equipment.
- Shake the tray side to side for 15 seconds and note what fails.
- Add cross-bracing (X shapes) on the walls and a wider base, then test again.
- Try a "base isolation" design by putting the tower on a few marbles inside a lid.
w: Earthquakes shake the ground sideways. Diagonal braces stop walls from folding into parallelograms, and wide bases resist tipping. Some real skyscrapers sit on rubber pads or sliding bearings so the ground moves under them.
x: Compare a tall skinny tower and a short wide one built from the same materials.

# Archimedes Screw
e=🌀 a=9-13 d=Medium t=45 min

- Wrap the tubing in a spiral around the bottle, keeping the turns evenly spaced.
- Tape it firmly in several places.
- Hold the bottle at about a 45 degree angle with the bottom tube end dipped in the water.

- Watch water climb the spiral and pour into the cup at the top.
