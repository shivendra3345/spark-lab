# Shake-a-Jar Sediment Layers
e=🫙 a=5-11 d=Easy t=1 day
m: clear jar with lid; gravel; sand; soil; clay; water
- Put a few handfuls of gravel, sand, soil and clay in the jar.
- Fill it almost to the top with water.
- Screw the lid on and shake hard for 1 minute.
- Set it down and watch the layers settle.
- Check again the next day and draw the layers.
w: Heavy pieces like gravel sink first. Fine clay settles last. This is how sedimentary rock layers form over millions of years.
x: Measure each layer and compare with soil from your yard.

# Crayon Rock Cycle
e=🖍️ a=8-13 d=Medium t=1 hour
m: old crayons; pencil sharpener or grater; foil; hot water or sunlight; book
- Shave crayons into small pieces (weathering).
- Pile them in foil and press with a book (sedimentary rock).
- Fold the foil and warm it in hot water to soften (metamorphic rock).
- An adult melts some completely and lets them cool (igneous rock).
w: Rocks constantly change from one type to another through weathering, pressure, heat and melting. This is the rock cycle.
x: Make a poster showing each stage with your crayon samples.
s: Adults handle melting.

# String Stalactites
e=🦇 a=7-12 d=Easy t=1 week
m: 2 jars; Epsom salt; warm water; thick yarn; paper clips; plate
- Fill two jars with warm water and stir in Epsom salt until no more dissolves.
- Place a plate between the jars.
- Weigh each end of the yarn with paper clips and drop one end in each jar.
- Let the yarn sag over the plate.
- Watch stalactites form over a week.
w: The salt water travels along the yarn and drips. As water evaporates, salt crystals build up like real cave stalactites.
x: Compare Epsom salt and baking soda.

# Plaster Fossil Molds and Casts
e=🦴 a=7-12 d=Medium t=1 hour
m: modeling clay; shells; plaster of Paris; water; paper cups; petroleum jelly
- Press a shell into flat clay to make a mold.
- Rub on a little petroleum jelly.
- Mix plaster and pour it into the mold.

w: Real fossils are often molds (an empty impression) or casts (where minerals filled the mold).

# Runny vs Thick Lava
e=🌋 a=8-12 d=Easy t=30 min
m: honey; ketchup; dish soap; water; cutting board for a ramp; books; timer; wax paper
- Cover a cutting board with wax paper and prop one end on books.
- Put one spoon of each liquid in a row at the top.
- Start the timer as you let them go.
- Record how long each takes to reach the bottom.
- Draw the shape each one makes.
w: Thick lava flows slowly and piles up into steep volcanoes. Runny lava spreads out into wide, gently sloping shield volcanoes like those in Hawaii.
x: Warm the honey in warm water and test again. Hotter lava is runnier.

# Rock Scratch Test
e=💎 a=8-13 d=Easy t=30 min
m: 6-10 different rocks and minerals; your fingernail; copper coin; steel nail; glass tile with taped edges; notebook
- Try to scratch each rock with your fingernail (hardness about 2.5).
- If it doesn't scratch, try the copper coin (about 3.5).
- Then try the steel nail (about 5.5).
- Then see if the rock can scratch the glass (about 5.5).
- Rank your rocks from softest to hardest.
w: Mohs hardness scale ranks minerals from 1 (talc) to 10 (diamond). A harder mineral always scratches a softer one, which helps geologists identify minerals.
x: Find a rock that scratches glass. It's likely quartz.
s: An adult should hold the glass tile.

# Mineral Streak Test
e=🪨 a=8-13 d=Easy t=20 min
m: unglazed white tile (back of a bathroom tile); minerals like hematite, pyrite, graphite and quartz; notebook
- Rub each mineral firmly across the rough back of the tile.
- Look at the color of the powder line.
- Compare the streak with the outside color of the mineral.
- Record your findings in a table.
w: A mineral's streak shows its true powder color, which is often different from its outside. Shiny gold pyrite leaves a greenish-black streak, and silvery hematite leaves a red-brown one.
x: Use a mineral guide to identify a mystery rock.

# Soil Percolation Test
e=🌧️ a=7-12 d=Easy t=30 min
m: 3 plastic cups with holes poked in the bottom; sand; clay; potting soil; 3 clear jars; measuring cup; timer
- Fill each cup with a different soil, to the same level.
- Rest each cup on top of a clear jar.
- Pour 1 cup of water into each and start the timer.
- Record when water starts and stops dripping.
- Measure how much water came through each.
w: Sandy soil has large gaps, so water rushes through. Clay has tiny particles packed tightly, so water drains slowly. Farmers and builders need to know how fast soil drains.
x: Mix equal parts of each soil to make loam and test it.

# Geyser Model
e=⛲ a=8-13 d=Medium t=30 min
m: large pot; small metal funnel that fits inside; water; stove (adult)
- An adult places the funnel upside down in the pot.
- Fill the pot with water until the funnel is just covered.
- An adult heats it slowly on the stove.
- Watch from a safe distance as bubbles build up under the funnel.

w: In real geysers, underground water is heated by hot rock. Steam bubbles build up pressure until water bursts out, then the cycle starts again.

# Clay Topographic Map
e=🗺️ a=9-13 d=Medium t=1 hour
m: modeling clay; fishing line or dental floss; ruler; toothpicks; paper; pencil
- Build a small mountain with one steep side and one gentle side.
- Push a toothpick straight down through the top to mark a center line.
- Use fishing line to slice the mountain into layers 1 cm thick.
- Trace the bottom layer on paper, then stack each layer and trace around it, lining up the toothpick hole.
- Compare the lines on the steep side with the gentle side.
w: Topographic maps show height using contour lines. Lines close together mean a steep slope, and lines far apart mean a gentle slope.
x: Find a real topographic map of a park near you.

# Simple Seismograph
e=📈 a=9-13 d=Medium t=45 min
m: cardboard box; paper cup; string; marker; long strip of paper; tape; small weights
- Cut the box open on one side.
- Hang a cup from the top with string, with weights inside.
- Poke a marker through the cup bottom so the tip just touches the box floor.
- Slide a paper strip under the marker.
- Pull the strip slowly while a friend shakes the table.
w: Seismographs record ground movement. The heavy cup stays still while the paper moves with the ground, so the marker draws the shaking.
x: Compare gentle and strong shakes.
